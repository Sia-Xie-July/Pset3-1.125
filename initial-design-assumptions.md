# Step 2 — Initial Design Assumptions

This concept proposes a shared AI datacenter for a hypothetical consortium of Finnish universities and educational institutions in Kajaani, Kainuu, Finland. The exact site has not been selected. Finland is the proposed design case; Canada (Québec) and Singapore are comparison cases.

The following values are planning assumptions and design targets, not verified site capabilities or a commitment to construction. Unknown values remain unknown rather than being entered as zero.

## Baseline

The assignment baseline is retained to provide a consistent starting point for comparing build, lease, and phased hybrid options. Retaining 25 MW does not establish that university demand justifies this scale.

| Variable | Initial value | Classification | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| IT load | 20 MW | Assignment baseline / assumption | Aggregate design load for computing, storage, and IT networking; not GPU power alone. |
| PUE (Power Usage Effectiveness) | 1.25 | Assignment baseline / target | Assumed ratio of total facility electricity to IT electricity. Actual performance requires a load- and weather-dependent model. |
| Facility electrical load | 25 MW | Calculation | 20 MW × 1.25; includes IT equipment, cooling, electrical losses, and other facility demand. |
| Annual operating hours | 8,760 hours | Assignment baseline / assumption | A 365-day operating-year basis; not a guarantee of uninterrupted service or productive GPU utilization. |
| Annual facility electricity | 219 GWh/year | Calculation | 25 MW × 8,760 hours ÷ 1,000, assuming continuous operation at the baseline load. This is not a measured consumption forecast. |

Actual annual energy will subsequently be calculated from the operating load profile and cooling demand. GPU utilization will be modelled separately; electricity consumption will not simply be multiplied by GPU utilization because idle equipment and facility systems still consume power.

If PUE rises to 1.40, a 20 MW IT load requires 28 MW of facility power. If the facility remains capped at 25 MW, IT load must instead fall to approximately 17.9 MW, or the design must change.

## Initial Design Assumptions

| Required item | Proposed assumption or design decision | Evidence and verification needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Selected country and region** | **Kajaani, Kainuu, Finland.** Investigate a site near suitable electricity and fiber infrastructure. No land, existing facility, or infrastructure access is assumed to have been secured. | LUMI demonstrates an existing research-computing and warm-water-cooling application in Kajaani [S1]. Verify land availability, permits, network routes, university demand, and whether existing compute could meet that demand. |
| **Grid connection concept** | Use the Finnish electricity grid as the primary supply. Request a connection supporting the 25 MW facility demand. Evaluate two supply routes and separate downstream distribution paths, aiming to preserve required capacity after the largest electrical component fails. Connection voltage, transformer ratings, and topology remain undetermined. | Obtain a site-specific utility connection assessment: capacity, voltage, upgrades, cost, energization date, and common failure points. Two cables do not establish independent supplies. Fingrid reports regional connection queues [S2]; existing infrastructure at another facility does not prove capacity is available to this project. |
| **Backup-power concept** | Adopt a **10-minute UPS design target** for the protected IT, networking, controls, and cooling systems, followed by standby diesel generation. Use modular generation with N+1 redundancy: after the largest generator fails, remaining usable capacity must support the 25 MW baseline facility demand. Set a **48-hour full-load backup target**, subject to fuel and equipment verification. | The conservative UPS energy basis is 25 MW × 10/60 hours ≈ **4.17 MWh delivered**, before losses, aging, and reserve allowances. The 48-hour load-energy basis is **1,200 MWh**. Verify generator output under site conditions, startup and switching time, cooling continuity, fuel consumption and storage, permits, and delivery resilience. These calculations do not prove uptime or determine fuel volume. |
| **Cooling approach** | Use **direct-to-chip liquid cooling for high-density GPU racks**, with contained air cooling for components that still require it. Transfer heat through coolant distribution units and a closed facility loop to outdoor dry coolers. Use ambient conditions when sufficient; allow supplemental air-cooled mechanical refrigeration when needed. Provide redundant pumps and cooling modules. Treat heat recovery as optional. | DOE guidance supports evaluating direct liquid cooling, dry heat rejection, and heat reuse [S3]; LUMI provides a local example [S1]. Verify GPU compatibility, rack heat density, permitted coolant temperatures, hourly weather, extreme summer conditions, winter freeze protection, and operation after the largest cooling module fails. No year-round chiller-free operation is assumed. |
| **Water requirements** | Prefer a design without routine evaporative cooling towers. Use treated coolant in closed loops with leak detection and a separately controlled outdoor loop suitable for freezing conditions. Provide water for initial filling, maintenance, any makeup needs, sanitation, and fire protection as required. | Annual water consumption, peak supply demand, and water-use effectiveness remain **unknown**, not zero. Obtain equipment fluid volumes, makeup estimates, water quality requirements, fire-water requirements, and a local water-service assessment. If evaporative equipment becomes necessary, update both the water and energy models. |
| **Network-connectivity requirements** | Plan **two physically diverse external fiber connections, each provisionally 100 Gbit/s**, to support university access and dataset transfers; this bandwidth is an assumption, not a verified requirement or available service. Provide a separate internal high-bandwidth, low-latency GPU fabric sized to the selected cluster and storage workloads. | Obtain carrier and research-network availability, route maps, prices, latency measurements, and transfer-demand estimates. Verify that one external link can support the agreed essential workload after the other fails. Determine internal fabric and storage throughput from actual training workloads rather than external-link speed. |
| **Expected electricity sources** | Purchase grid electricity, whose mix includes nuclear power and renewables. Evaluate renewable electricity contracts where practical. Assume no dedicated onsite solar, wind, or gas generation in the initial operating case; standby diesel is used for testing and outages. | Statistics Finland reports **95% fossil-free domestic electricity production in 2024**, including nuclear, with **57% renewable production** [S4]. These are annual national production figures, not this facility's hourly supply or emissions. Verify tariff, procurement terms, imports, and hourly carbon data; do not claim continuous renewable supply or zero emissions. |
| **Major engineering uncertainties** | The main unresolved issues are site-specific grid capacity and timing; electrical failure paths; actual PUE across loads and seasons; cooling performance and water demand; 48-hour backup capability; and fiber diversity. Demand and GPU selection may change the required infrastructure scale. | Resolve the issues using utility assessments, equipment specifications, hourly thermal and electrical models, fuel studies, network surveys, and member workload commitments before treating the concept as feasible. |

The preferred grid/UPS/generator and liquid-cooling arrangements are proposed choices, not evidence that the design meets an uptime requirement. The consortium must agree workload-specific availability targets. Reliability verification requires failure and maintenance data, a complete electrical and cooling diagram, protection and switching studies, and commissioning tests.

If power arrives one year late, the initial contingency is to delay full operation and evaluate temporary leased compute rather than assume year-round operation on standby generators. If demand proves insufficient, reduce or phase the proposed IT capacity.
